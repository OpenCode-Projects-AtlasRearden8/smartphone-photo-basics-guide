# Portraits That Flatter

Making someone look like the best version of their ordinary self is a
kindness, and it is a learnable one. None of it requires portrait mode
(though it helps). All of it requires paying attention to the person
instead of the phone.

## Start with light on the face — again

Everything from the [light chapter](01-light-without-jargon.md) applies
twice to faces:

- **Window light, subject facing it** — a face lit by soft window
  light simply looks better. Skin evens out, eyes gain that bright
  catchlight that makes portraits feel alive.
- **Never top light for faces.** The sun directly overhead carves deep
  shadows into eye sockets. Move your person into the shade of a
  building with open sky in front of them — "open shade" is the single
  most flattering light you can find anywhere on a sunny day.
- **Backlight for a glow.** Sunset behind a person, faces turned
  sideways so light brushes their cheeks: dreamy and forgiving. Tap on
  the face so the phone keeps their skin correct instead of turning
  the whole frame dark.

## Where to hold the camera

This is the highest-leverage portrait tip in the manual:

- **For most people, camera at their chest or collarbone height, you a
  step or two back.** This is the honest, natural vantage. Hitting
  eye level or slightly below eye level for seated subjects is also
  kind.
- **Camera slightly above chest height, angled gently down** is
  famously flattering for *almost everyone* — it slims and defines.
  It is why group photos at parties get shot over the heads of the crowd.
- **Never shoot up at a face from chin height** unless you want the
  jaw, the nostrils, and the ceiling to share the photo. Upward
  angles are for buildings and card towers, not faces.

## Distance: a body needs air, a face needs none

- **Full-body portrait:** leave breathing room above the head and
  below the feet, and turn the body about a quarter-turn from the
  camera — bodies photograph nicer angled than square-on, and a
  hand in a pocket instantly relaxes a pose.
- **Head portrait:** get closer than feels comfortable, right up to
  about two steps away. Cutting off the very top of the hair is
  acceptable; cutting off a chin is not.

## Pose people with conversation, not commands

"Say cheese" produces teeth. Talk creates faces. While composing, just
chat — "what was the best part of your day?" — and press the shutter
*at the end of a sentence*, when the laugh is real. A burst of three
photos straddles the laugh, and one of the three will be a keeper.

If you direct at all, direct with actions, not shapes: "look at the
sea for a second", "walk toward me slowly", "put your hand on their
shoulder." People know what walking and hugging look like; nobody
knows what "relax your chin" means.

## Groups without grievances

1. **Two rows maximum:** taller folks behind, seated or crouched in
   front. A bench is a gift.
2. **Hands do something** — pockets, shoulders, hug. Floating arms
   read as awkward.
3. **Take four: one "everyone serious," two "laughing," one "anyone —
   go."** Somewhere in the sequence is the photo that becomes the
   Christmas card.
4. **On a timer for the photographer's inclusion:** prop the phone,
   10-second timer, walk in. Last one in the frame, first to laugh —
   this is usually the best photo of the day.

## Portrait mode, used gently

Portrait mode fakes a soft background to make the subject pop. It is
wonderful and it has two failure modes: it mishandles fine edges
(glasses, flyaway hair, chair slats) and it tempts people to use it at
party distance for landscapes. Use it when the subject is within a few
steps of you; check the edges before you walk away; retake if the
phone scribbled around their hair.

## The kindness checklist for portraits

- Light on their face, not up their nose.
- Camera above chest height.
- You talked, they laughed, you caught it.
- Full-body got air, close-up got close.
- You checked the background (the [framing chapter](03-framing-rules-you-can-bend.md) handles that).

Do those five and the person in the photo will thank you — probably
at the next birthday, holding the print.
