Steady Hands

Blur is the most common reason a phone photo gets deleted. The good news: the cure costs nothing, because the steadiest camera support you own is already attached to your shoulders.

Why phone photos blur

A phone camera works like your eyes in a dim room: when light is scarce, it keeps the shutter open slightly longer to drink in every drop. If your hands drift while it is "looking," your whole photo smears. So blur is mostly a story about light and time, not about your equipment.

That means the light chapter you just read was also the blur chapter you just read. More light = shorter time = sharper photo. When you cannot add light, you add stillness instead. That is this chapter.

How to hold a phone like it matters

The one-handed selfie-grip is the leakiest way to hold anything. Instead, make a small pocket of your own two hands:

  1. Two hands, always. Hold the phone with your right hand, cradle it with the fingers of your left. Elbows stay tucked against your ribs — tucked elbows are a tripod with a heartbeat.
  2. Exhale, then press. Breathe out slowly and press the shutter at the bottom of the breath. This is borrowed straight from marksmanship and it works just as well for portraits.
  3. Shutter button or volume button — whichever steadier. On most phones the volume-up button also takes the photo, and tapping it lets you keep both elbows tucked instead of stretching a thumb.
  4. Lean on something. Wall, doorframe, park bench, a friend's shoulder. Contact with almost anything solid removes most of your wobble.

The timer trick everyone forgets

Pressing the button is itself a small shove. Set a 3-second timer and your hands are resting flat when the photo happens, not mid-tap. Use it for:

  • group photos, where you are also in the frame or trying to be quick,
  • any photo in a dim room,
  • and the moments (candles being blown out) where you want to be watching the moment, not the phone.

When things move, outsmart the motion

You cannot hold still a moving toddler or a dachshund mid-zoomies. Instead of fighting, change the game:

  • Shoot a short burst (typically hold the shutter button, or use burst mode). Ten frames later, one catches the grin and the ears both up. Delete the rest without guilt.
  • Track, then wait. Keep the phone pointed at where the action is heading and let the motion come to your frame, instead of swinging the phone to chase it. The swing itself causes blur.
  • Brighter is faster. A mover photographed near a bright window will come out sharper than in a dark corner. Drag the action into the light when you can.

The table, the bookshelf, the Mini tripod you already own

No gear is required here, but the world is full of accidental tripods: a stack of hardcover books, an upside-down mug, a windowsill, a car dashboard, a railing. Rest the phone on anything with a small object (a folded receipt, a rubber band) as a tilt shim. That is how every great shelf photo in your future gets taken.

For group photos you need to be in: prop the phone on the table with a timer and walk into frame. It is easier than handing your phone to a stranger and hoping.

Is it blur, or is it focus?

Before deleting a soft photo, zoom in.

  • The whole frame is smeared in one direction — that is hand or motion blur. Slow down, add light, retry.
  • The background is sharp but the face is soft — the phone focused on the wrong patch. Tap your subject's face on the screen before shooting; the focus square will hop over to them.
  • Everything crisp but nostalgic-soft glow — that might be the phone's "beauty"/smoothing filter quietly on. Check your camera app settings; turn it down or off. Many people discover their camera has been airbrushing their whole family for a year.
