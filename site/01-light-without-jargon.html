Light Without Jargon

If you only improve one thing about your photos, improve this. Photographs are literally pictures of light. Great phone photos are rarely about a better camera — they are about standing in better light.

And "better light" needs no physics. Here is the plain-English version.

The four honest rules of light

1. Turn your subject toward the light, not away from it

Indoors, that usually means: put the person near a window and you stay near the window too, so the window light falls on their face — not behind them. Outdoors, face them toward the open sky, not toward the sun blazing straight into their eyes.

Try it today: take one photo of a person with the window behind them, then move so the window is behind you. Compare. This one habit will transform more of your photos than any camera setting ever could.

2. Window light is your best friend — treat it like a soft lamp

A window is a huge, gentle light source. Light through a window on an overcast day is the kindest portrait light in existence, and it comes free with every building you will ever stand in.

  • Put your subject within arm's reach of the window, facing it.
  • If the light makes one half of the face darker than you like, that is fine — that gentle shadow is what makes faces look three-dimensional.

3. Overcast days are quietly wonderful

Gray days get blamed unfairly. Clouds turn the whole sky into one giant softbox, which means soft shadows, honest colors, and no squinting. Some of the most flattering photos ever taken of people happened on days when everyone complained about the weather.

If it is overcast and you were planning to take portraits at the park — do not postpone. Go.

4. Turn off the flash by default

The built-in flash is a tiny, harsh burst of light right next to the lens. It flattens faces, washes out skin, and makes everything look like a security camera. On the few phones where the flash is decent, it is still only for emergencies.

Default: flash off. If the room is too dark, do not reach for the flash — reach for these, in order:

  1. Move your subject toward an existing light (window, lamp).
  2. Steady yourself (next chapter) so the phone can let in more light with a longer exposure.
  3. Tap the bright/dark slider (sun icon or brightness line) and slide it up a touch — modern phones can see in the dark better than you would think.

The flash earns its keep in exactly two situations: someone is moving fast in a dim room, and you need a photo even a bad one, or you are trying to find your keys at the bottom of a bag. That is it.

The golden hour, without the mysticism

The hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset is called golden hour because everything it touches glows warm and gentle — faces, sidewalks, buildings, pets. It is the easiest beautiful light there is, and it happens every day for free.

You do not need a photography schedule for it. Just notice: if you happen to be outside around sunset, that is the moment to take the photo you were going to take anyway.

Watch for light problems you can walk out of

Before you press the button, glance at your photo's light for these three culprits — and if you see one, take two steps sideways and shoot again:

  • Harsh top-down sun (midday) — makes raccoon-eyes shadows under brows and noses. Step into open shade: the edge of a building's shadow, an awning over a shop door, or the rim of a tree's shade all work.
  • Your own shadow falling across the subject. Pivot.
  • Weird orange indoor light mixing with bluish window light on the same face. Pick one: either move fully toward the window or fully away, so there is one story about light on your subject.

The one-minute version

Turn toward light. Keep flash off. Cloudy days are gifts. Golden hour is free. When in doubt, take one step toward a window.

That is the whole chapter. You now know more practical light than most people who have owned a camera for ten years.
